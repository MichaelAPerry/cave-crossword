## 2. Answers to (a)-(d)

(a) Fill success/solvability vs dictionary size or grid density: YES, measured. Works 1 and 2 give P(soluble) and %SAT vs dictionary fraction (1-100%) and number of blocked cells, for 6x6, 9x9, and 15-23 grids, with a real English (UK) list subsampled at random. Eckler/Long (3, 4) give expected-count and support thresholds for full word squares (no black squares). Limits: dictionary is varied only by random subsampling of one list; 9x9 sample is median-based with 18.5% timeouts (solvability probability excludes failed runs); the 23x23 study is a handful of instances per region, no fine curves; no finite-size scaling, no threshold-width/exponent estimate.

(b) Easy-hard-easy / threshold for crosswords or word squares: YES for crosswords (works 1, 2: hard region at the SAT/UNSAT boundary, 2D version over dict x blocks; hard region widens with grid size). For word squares the "support" (Eckler) and E(F)=1 criterion (Long) is the expected-solution-count threshold; no sharpness analysis (no probability-vs-size curves with width scaling) found. "Sharp" in the sense of width -> 0 with grid size was not tested; 15x15 -> 23x23 hard region widened, which is in tension with a sharpening claim and is unexplained.

(c) Compare lexicons/languages for fillability: NOT found. Only one language (UK English list). Long's 1.5x observed/theory support ratio on real lists is the only real-vs-model comparison, and the model is an independent-letter (frequency-only) null, not a Markov lexicon. Botea & Anbulagan explicitly state the independence estimate of p_i fails because of letter-frequency non-uniformity and leave a better p_i open.

(d) Estimates of number of solutions/fills: YES. Botea & Anbulagan 2009 (E(N) formula with fitted p_i; matches exact 6x6 counts over 8 orders of magnitude; Table 1), Long 1993 (closed form for squares; extrapolated totals e.g. ~240M 5-squares from 16,536 words), Knuth/forum counts for 5x5 (secondhand). No parameter-free estimator that predicts p_i from lexicon statistics (e.g. order-2 stats) is known to me.

## 3. Verdict: PARTIALLY DONE

Done: the existence of an easy-hard-easy / SAT-UNSAT transition for crossword fill vs dictionary size and vs number of blocks (Anbulagan & Botea 2008; Botea & Anbulagan 2009), plus a fitted expected-solution-count model. Word-square support thresholds (Eckler 1992, Long 1993).

Open (appears novel):
1. Real vs synthetic lexicon with matched letter statistics (Markov order-2 or higher) and the resulting shift of the threshold: no prior work found.
2. Cross-language / cross-lexicon fillability comparison.
3. Minimum word length as an explicit axis (only incidental: 6x6/9x9 grids avoid 2-letter slots by design; not varied).
4. Sharpness: finite-size scaling, threshold width vs grid size, critical exponent; crisp probability curves with many instances (existing 9x9 curves exclude timeouts, 23x23 sample tiny). The widening hard region with grid size contradicts naive sharpening, worth explaining.
5. A predictive p_i (intersection compatibility) derived from lexicon statistics (e.g. via order-2 letter transitions, positional letter stats, or word-length-specific statistics), rather than fitted per grid; and an annealed vs quenched comparison of E(N) vs true P(sat) (E(N)=1 as threshold predictor, first-moment vs actual).
6. Dictionary variation by something other than uniform subsampling (frequency-ranked cut, weighted lists, per-length caps), and word reuse allowed/forbidden, and slot-length distribution effects.

## 4. Must cite / do differently

Must cite: Anbulagan & Botea 2008; Botea & Anbulagan 2009 (closest; claim of "first multi-dimensional phase transition for crosswords" is theirs); Eckler 1992 and Long 1993 (support, independent-letter model, 1.5x real-list factor); Beacham et al. 2001, Ginsberg et al. 1990, Ginsberg 2011 (benchmark and solver context); Gourves et al. 2021 (NP-hardness; Garey-Johnson/Lewis-Papadimitriou); Williams & Hogg 1992 (mean hardness = space/solutions, cited in Botea 2009; unverified here); classic random-CSP transition papers (unverified here); Hartmann 2024 only to disambiguate "crossword percolation".

Do differently: (i) use exact or well-validated counting (not wall-clock timeouts) so P(fillable) has no censored mass; use many random grids per openness level and report threshold location and width with finite-size scaling across grid sizes; (ii) make min word length and slot-length distribution explicit axes; (iii) build synthetic lexicons matched on length distribution and letter unigram/bigram (and positional) statistics, sampled at identical sizes, and compare E(N) and P(sat) curves to the real lexicon, quantifying the threshold shift in "words per length" units; (iv) test multiple natural languages; (v) report predictors (first-moment E(N)=1, Long's p, fitted p_i) versus measured threshold; (vi) fix the word-reuse convention and state it, since Botea's model includes repetition constraints.

Caveats: Ginsberg 1990, Beacham 2001, Dr.Fill were not read for threshold content (only bibliographic verification), so a small chance remains they contain dictionary-size observations; Eckler's full text unread.
